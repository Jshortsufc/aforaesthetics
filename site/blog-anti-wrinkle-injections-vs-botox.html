Anti-wrinkle injections vs "Botox": what's the difference?

If you've researched frown-line or forehead treatments, you'll have seen the word "Botox" everywhere — and "anti-wrinkle injections" alongside it. Here's what each term actually means, how the treatment works, and why the wording matters more than you'd think.

"Botox" is a brand, not the treatment

Botox is a registered brand name — one of several licensed products made from botulinum toxin. It became so widely known that people now use it as a catch-all, the way "Hoover" came to mean any vacuum cleaner. The treatment itself is more accurately called anti-wrinkle or wrinkle-relaxing injections, and it can use any one of a number of licensed botulinum toxin products, chosen by your prescriber.

How anti-wrinkle injections work

Certain lines — frown lines between the brows, forehead lines, crow's feet around the eyes — are created by repeated muscle movement over the years. A very small amount of botulinum toxin, placed precisely, relaxes the specific muscles responsible, so the overlying skin creases less and the lines soften. It develops gradually over one to two weeks and typically lasts a few months before naturally wearing off. Done well and conservatively, the aim is a rested, natural look that still moves — not a frozen one.

Why you won't see reputable clinics advertise "Botox" by name

Here's the part most people don't know. Botulinum toxin is a prescription-only medicine, and in the UK it's against the rules to advertise prescription-only medicines to the public. That's why careful, compliant clinics talk about "anti-wrinkle injections" rather than promoting a brand — it isn't coyness, it's the law, enforced by the ASA and the MHRA. If a clinic is happily advertising a prescription medicine by brand to win your click, it's worth asking what else they're relaxed about.

What matters far more than the brand: who treats you

Because it's a prescription-only medicine, anti-wrinkle treatment should always be assessed and prescribed by a qualified prescriber after a proper face-to-face consultation — not sold like a beauty product. The single most important decision you make isn't which brand of toxin, but who is holding the needle and whether they can prescribe, recognise when a treatment isn't right for you, and manage the rare occasions something needs attention.

Is it safe?

In trained, qualified hands and following a proper assessment, anti-wrinkle injections are a well-established treatment with a long track record. Temporary side effects such as minor swelling, redness or bruising at the injection site can happen and usually settle quickly. The biggest risks come not from the treatment itself but from unqualified people offering it cheaply — which is exactly why prescriber-led care matters.

Common questions

Is Botox the same as anti-wrinkle injections?

Botox is one well-known brand of botulinum toxin, the prescription-only medicine used in anti-wrinkle injections. "Anti-wrinkle injections" is the general term for the treatment, which can use any of several licensed products.

Why don't UK clinics advertise Botox by name?

Botulinum toxin is a prescription-only medicine, and UK rules prohibit advertising prescription-only medicines to the public. Reputable clinics therefore describe the treatment generically rather than promoting a brand.

How long do the results last?

Results usually develop over one to two weeks and commonly last around three to four months, though it varies from person to person.

Who should carry it out?

Because it's prescription-only, it should be assessed and prescribed by a qualified prescriber after a face-to-face consultation. A nurse prescriber can both prescribe and administer it.
